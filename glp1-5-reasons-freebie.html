5 reasons women gain weight back after stopping a GLP-1

And what you can do about it. A practical guide for women who want lasting freedom around food.

Georgie Beames · Psychologist & Coach · 15 years in eating, weight and GLP-1 medications

First, let's clear something up

If you're taking a GLP-1 and your hunger has reduced, but you still find yourself thinking about food, reaching for food when you're not hungry, or eating to soothe yourself, you are not failing.

And it doesn't necessarily mean your medication isn't working.

GLP-1 medications can have a powerful effect on appetite and hunger. But food isn't always about hunger.

Sometimes food has become a way to create comfort, relief, reward, safety or a break from the demands of your day.

That's the part medication can't necessarily address.

And that's good news, because once you understand what's driving your food noise, you can start working on it.

This is for you if you're taking a GLP-1 and:

  • Your physical hunger has reduced, but you still think about food.
  • You sometimes want to eat when you know you're not physically hungry.
  • You find yourself eating for comfort, reward, relaxation or relief.
  • Your food noise gets louder when you're stressed, overwhelmed, tired or emotionally activated.
  • You worry about what will happen when you eventually reduce or stop your GLP-1.
  • You want to understand why you're still experiencing food noise, rather than simply trying harder to control it.
  • You want practical tools to help you feel calmer and more in control around food.

If your appetite has eased but food still feels like it's taking up too much space in your mind, you're in the right place.

01

Your hunger can settle while your food noise keeps going.

One of the most frustrating experiences can be: "I'm barely hungry, so why am I still thinking about food?"

You may have less physical hunger, feel fuller sooner and have less appetite, but still find yourself thinking about chocolate, grazing in the evening, opening the pantry or planning what you're going to eat later.

That's because not all food noise is hunger. Sometimes the urge to eat is connected to an emotional or nervous-system pattern that existed long before you started your GLP-1. Food may have become associated with comfort, relaxation, reward, relief, safety, connection or escape.

So when the physical hunger has eased, you may finally have the opportunity to notice what was underneath it.

Try this

The next time you want to eat when you're not physically hungry, don't immediately try to stop yourself. Instead ask:

"What am I hoping this food will do for me right now?"

Maybe you want to relax. Maybe you want some time to yourself. Maybe you're overwhelmed, or bored, or looking for comfort. There is no right answer. You're simply gathering information.

02

Your cravings usually have a trigger.

Food noise often seems to appear out of nowhere. But when you start paying attention, you'll often notice a pattern. A stressful email. Walking through the front door after a long day. Feeling overwhelmed. Being alone. 3pm. 9pm. A difficult conversation. Feeling criticised. Feeling like you've failed.

Your nervous system is constantly asking one question: "Am I safe?" You can think of it as having three broad states.

SafeGrounded, connected, present and regulated.
AnxiousStressed, alert, overwhelmed, perfectionistic or "on".
FreezeNumb, stuck, disconnected, exhausted or wanting to escape.

Cravings can become louder when something tips you out of the safe state and into anxiety or freeze. This is why you can sometimes be barely hungry but suddenly desperate to eat.

Try this

The next time food noise gets loud, pause and ask:

"Which state am I in right now? Safe, anxious, or freeze?"

You don't have to change anything. Just notice. Awareness is the first step towards choice.

03

Sometimes you're not relaxing, you're numbing.

This one can be a big aha moment. You finish your day. You sit on the couch. You have something to eat. You scroll your phone. You watch television. And you tell yourself: "This is my time to relax."

But sometimes what looks like relaxation is actually numbing. Numbing is disconnecting from what you're feeling. True relaxation feels different. It's being present. Connected. Grounded. Actually feeling your body soften.

Food can give you a brief feeling of relief while leaving the underlying stress or emotion untouched.

Try this · start with 2 minutes

When you notice you're using food, scrolling or TV to switch off, pause and give yourself two minutes of genuine rest instead. You could sit somewhere calm, step outside, take a few slow breaths, stretch, listen to one song, make a cup of tea, or simply do nothing.

The goal isn't to replace food forever. You're simply practising the difference between numbing and genuinely feeling more settled.

04

The medication can settle your hunger, but it can't change every food pattern.

If you're genuinely not hungry on your GLP-1, you're not necessarily restricting. Your appetite may simply be much lower. But you can still find yourself thinking about food, craving something specific, eating for comfort, or reaching for food when you're already physically satisfied.

That's because hunger and food noise aren't always the same thing. Your medication can influence appetite and fullness, while the emotional and nervous-system patterns underneath your eating can still be there.

In fact, the reduction in physical hunger can give you something incredibly valuable: space to finally notice what's underneath the eating.

Try this

When you notice yourself wanting food despite not being physically hungry, pause and ask:

"If I stopped and sat with this feeling instead of eating, what might I have to feel?"

You don't need to solve it. You don't even need to change what you do next. Just notice. That awareness is the beginning of changing the pattern.

05

You don't need to fight the craving, you need to create a pause.

When a craving hits, it can feel like you have only two options. Eat it, or fight it. But there is another option. Pause.

An urge is a wave. It rises. It peaks. And it changes. You don't have to obey it, and you don't have to fight it. You can create a little space between the urge and what you do next.

Try this · the longer exhale

When food noise gets loud:

Breathe in for 4 secondsBreathe out for 6 to 8 secondsRepeat 3 to 4 times

Then ask: "What do I need right now?"

You can still choose to eat. The goal isn't to control yourself. The goal is to create enough space to make a conscious choice.

Your one action this week

Don't try to stop the food noise. Get curious about it.

For the next 7 days, every time you notice food noise when you're not physically hungry, write down three things.

  1. What happened just before the craving?What were you doing? Who were you with? What were you thinking or feeling?
  2. Which state am I in?Safe, anxious, or freeze.
  3. What am I hoping the food will do for me?Comfort me? Relax me? Reward me? Distract me? Help me feel safe? Give me some time for myself?

Then take three slow breaths with a longer exhale before deciding what to do. That's it. You don't need to be perfect. You're learning your pattern, and once you can see the pattern, you can start changing it.

The bigger picture

A GLP-1 can be incredibly helpful in settling hunger and appetite. But lasting freedom around food involves more than hunger. It involves learning to:

  • recognise your triggers
  • understand your food noise
  • regulate your nervous system
  • respond to emotions differently
  • create a pause between an urge and your response
  • build new ways of finding comfort, safety and relaxation

The goal isn't to become someone who never wants food. The goal is to become someone who can experience an urge without feeling controlled by it.

And eventually, the calm you feel around food becomes something you can create for yourself, rather than something you depend on medication to provide.
